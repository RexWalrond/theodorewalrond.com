Reading 300 Studies: How I Approach a Research Question

By Theodore Walrond

Two research projects shaped how I read evidence. At the University of Florida's McKnight Brain Institute, from 2022 to 2023, I worked through more than 300 studies on whether a subarachnoid hemorrhage can leave someone more vulnerable to Alzheimer's disease. Before that, in the summer of 2021, I tracked more than 25 COVID-19 drug candidates for Romark Laboratories in Tampa and reviewed over 400 articles on one older drug, niclosamide. Here's the approach I took away from both.

1. Pin down the question first

A broad topic produces an endless reading list. A specific question, such as whether one kind of brain injury predicts a later disease, tells you which papers matter and which can wait.

2. Organize before you interpret

At Romark the tool was a set of dashboards that logged every candidate by efficacy, trial phase and mechanism of action. Seeing those three things side by side made it obvious which drugs had both a plausible mechanism and maturing evidence. A clear structure surfaces patterns faster than reading alone.

3. Weigh studies by design, not by headline

An animal study, a small clinical series and a large patient follow-up can all point the same way and still deserve very different weight. Promising lab results and proven benefit in people are not the same thing.

4. Look for agreement, conflict and gaps

The most useful output of the McKnight review was not a single answer. It was a framework of the variables that seemed to connect vascular injury to neurodegeneration, such as inflammation, damage to the blood-brain barrier and slower clearance of waste from brain tissue, along with where the evidence was thin. Those gaps are what point to the next experiment.

5. Explain it to people outside your corner

Presenting the findings to faculty from three departments forced the framework to make sense to people who didn't share my reading list. If it can't survive that, it isn't finished.

I've since moved from research toward the business side of health, but this is still how I approach any hard question. A plain-language look at the brain-bleed question itself is on rexwalrond.com.
